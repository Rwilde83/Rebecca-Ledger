Three questions, one ledger

Who gets paid. Who owns it. Who's watching.

The second question

Pay is the part you can see. Ownership is where the money lives.

Cap every executive on this page at twenty times their median worker and you move roughly $150–200 billion a year worldwide, about a tenth of one percent of the global economy. Real, and worth doing, but not the whole story. A salary is labor income. Most extreme wealth is ownership: shares, dividends, capital gains. A pay cap does not touch it.

So the ledger is adding a second column: what share of each company is held by its rank-and-file employees. For most large companies the answer is close to zero. That is a number nobody publishes and everybody would understand.

The person who makes the coffee and the person who runs the company should live in the same economy. At 1,794 to 1, they don't.

Five ways ownership has already been reset somewhere

MechanismWhere it already worksWhat it does
Broad employee equityPublix, WinCo, John Lewis (UK), thousands of ESOPsEvery worker gets shares every year as part of pay. Nothing is taken; new ownership is created and spread.
Citizens' wealth fundAlaska Permanent Fund, Norway's sovereign fundThe public holds a slice of the economy and everyone gets the dividend.
Tax ownership like workProposed widely, partial in several countriesEqual rates on capital and labor income; a real inheritance tax above a very high threshold.
AntitrustUS 1911–1984, EU todayConcentrated ownership rides on concentrated markets. Break up the market, spread the ownership.
Workers on the boardGermany, above 2,000 employeesDoesn't change who owns shares. Changes who owns decisions.

The honest objections: capital taxes may reduce investment; wealth funds can be politicized; single-company employee stock is risky if the company fails, which is why Enron's workers were wiped out; founders who take large risks arguably earn large rewards. Serious versions of any reset are built around those, with diversified employee funds instead of single-company stock and thresholds high enough to touch only the top fraction of a percent.

AI companies audit each other. Citizens audit the AI. And exposing corruption pays better than hiding it.

The third question

Who watches

The gap on the ledger persists for one reason: nobody is required to close it and nobody is paid to expose it. Change both. Four layers, each proven somewhere, never assembled. This is also how you keep AI itself honest while it is still small enough to hold.

1

Three AI monitors watch each other and every large company

Three independent AI systems, run by organizations in different countries with different funding and different training. Each audits the other two, plus the payroll, executive pay, ownership, supplier contracts, and political spending of every company above a size threshold, in every country it sells in. Their auditing code is public. When they disagree, the disagreement is published too.

2

Citizens chosen by lottery check the AI

Panels of ordinary people selected at random from every participating country, not elected and not appointed. Short terms, paid well, rotated constantly, identities protected while serving. They can demand plain-language explanations, order re-analysis, and escalate findings. The AI cannot overrule them.

3

Whoever exposes corruption gets paid

Anyone, anywhere, who reveals concealment or wrongdoing receives 20 to 40 percent of the penalty, funded from the penalty itself. The largest payouts go to exposing collusion among the monitors or inside a citizen panel, because those are the failures that would break the system. Legal protection and income replacement come with it.

The US SEC whistleblower program has paid out billions since 2011 for exactly this reason: it made reporting fraud more profitable than participating in it.

4

Humans judge. The AI only finds and publishes.

No monitor imposes a penalty. Findings that survive citizen review go to courts and public enforcement bodies in the relevant country. The AI is the auditor. It is never the judge.

Why this reaches racism

The bottom of every ladder on this page is disproportionately Black, brown, immigrant, and women: cashiers, baristas, warehouse workers, and the people in the factories overseas. Raise the floor and those are the people raised. Publish pay by job and location and unequal pay for equal work has nowhere to hide. Pick the watchers by lottery from everyone and the system cannot quietly become one group watching itself. It doesn't end racism. It takes away the dark, and racism needs the dark.

The rules it would enforce

The monitors enforce whatever the public sets. This is the starting proposal. It applies to every company above the size threshold, in every country, including their suppliers.

TierRule
FloorA local living wage in every country of operation, suppliers included. Not the legal minimum: enough for housing, food, healthcare, and savings where the person actually lives.
FrontlineLiving wage plus meaningful equity. Full-time available to everyone who wants it.
SkilledMarket pay. Bands published by level and location.
ExecutivesTotal compensation capped at 15 to 20 times the company's global median employee.
InnovationRoyalties on specific products, paid to the people who built them, capped per person per breakthrough. Reward invention where it happens, not two levels up.
OwnershipBroad employee equity every year, held through a diversified fund so no worker's savings ride on one company.
Profit shareA fixed percentage of net income distributed to all employees.
TransparencyPay ratio and bands published every year, in plain language, in every language the company operates in.

Applied to Apple, the CEO goes from $74 million to about $3 million, every store worker gains $10 to $15k, every factory worker in the supply chain reaches a living wage, and the company stays the most profitable on earth. The cost fits inside one year of stock buybacks. The same arithmetic holds for nearly every company on the ledger.

Is anyone building this?

What exists

  • Companies use AI to audit themselves. The vendor works for the company.
  • Governments are regulating AI. They are not using it to audit corporations.
  • Whistleblower bounties exist and work at the SEC and a few other agencies.
  • Citizens' assemblies have run successfully in Ireland, France, and dozens of cities.
  • Pay ratios are public by law in the US and UK, buried in filings almost no one reads.

What is missing

  • A publicly owned AI that audits companies rather than serving them.
  • Any arrangement where AI labs monitor each other.
  • A connection between the bounty and the detector.
  • Anyone with the incentive to build it. The labs sell compliance tools to the companies they would be auditing.

Every component is proven. The assembly is unbuilt. That is either a reason it can't work, or a reason it hasn't been tried. This is the conversation that needs to be happening in public, now, while the choice is still open.

Known weak points, so you don't have to find them
  • All three major AI labs today share a country, investors, and a hiring pool. That is one faction, not three.
  • Correlated blind spots: AIs trained on similar data miss the same things. The citizen panels exist to catch this, and might not.
  • The system guarantees compliance, not fairness. If people agree on bad rules, it will enforce bad rules.
  • Capital can flow to countries that opt out. The only lever is market access: sell here, get audited here.
  • Public ownership only works if the public watches. Apathy beats every other failure mode.
  • The author is one person with a laptop, not an institution. That's either the weakness or the point.
